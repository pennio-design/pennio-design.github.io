01 Now casting

A ń wá onídìrí ìbílẹ̀ tó dáńgájíá.

We are looking for skilled traditional hairdressers, and for models to wear their work.

Ojú gbooro is what Yoruba people say to a hairdresser at work. Ojú Gbooro brings that greeting back into everyday life, and African hair back as culture.

HairdressersYou plait, thread or braid traditional styles and can show your work.
ModelsMen and women, 18 or older, ready to wear the work on camera.
02 The greeting
You say to the hairdresserOjú gbooro
The onídìrí answersÒòyà á yà

Òòyà, also called ìlarun, is the traditional comb an onídìrí works with: carved from wood, with three long teeth for parting and lifting the hair. Òòyà á yà means the comb will do its work well: the hair will come out right.

It is an everyday greeting, the kind Yoruba people give to anyone at their craft, like the traditional textile weaver at the loom. Ojú Gbooro brings it back for the people who make our hair.

03 The heritage record

Every style carries knowledge. The hands that make it, the names it answers to, the tools that shape it, and the people who taught it.

01 / NamesWhat the style is called

Local names and the meanings practitioners attach to them, recorded in the language they use.

02 / TechniqueHow the work is made

Parting, threading, plaiting, braiding, shaping and the sequence of work, described by the hands that know it.

03 / ToolsWhat the craft requires

Combs, thread, fingers and other tools, including the knowledge carried in how each is used.

04 / PeopleWho keeps it alive

Hairdressers, apprentices, elders and models, with their permission and their own accounts.

A record built with its practitioners. Names, histories and techniques will be checked with the people who carry them. Each contribution remains attributed to its source, and nothing is published without permission.
